Virtual shadow maps for a 14-year-old engine

A modern shadow path, virtual-shadow-map style, grafted onto a 14-year-old DirectX 11 engine, built years before the technique existed. From the outside, no source. A live build log: one milestone proven, the rest open.

The frame-cost diagnostic ended on a number: Guild Wars 2's ~14-year-old DirectX 11 engine spends about 4 ms per frame on shadows and recomputes about 76% of that redundantly, on a technique that predates the modern lineage of shadow methods. This is the response: take a modern virtual-shadow-map-style path and bring it to an engine that shipped long before such techniques existed, from the outside, without the source. Published mid-build, on purpose: one hard result, and a pile of open problems behind it.

The goal

Writing a shadow renderer is the easy part. The hard problem is to make a closed engine, one never designed to accept anything from outside, use a shadow result computed entirely by my code as if it were its own.

Proven

The engine now draws correct shadows from a source I supply. Its native shadow map is replaced by one my code produces, and the engine samples that instead. A user checked the visual result across every one of the engine's shadow compose modes and confirmed it correct in all of them.

Status

A build log, unretouched. Three things true at once.

It crashes. The integration produces correct output when it runs; keeping it running reliably is the live front. Today's problem is stability, not correctness of the image.

Nothing is measured. No performance number exists: you can't benchmark what isn't stable. When there's a measured number, there'll be a measured number.

Nothing is shipped. No studio relationship, no credit. Independent R&D, self-attested, mid-flight.

Method withheld

This log shows what was achieved and what remains, never how. It does not describe how an external shadow result is handed to the engine, how the engine is made to sample it instead of its own, which resource is substituted, or any of the hooking, interop, or addressing that makes the substitution possible. That mechanism is the commercial core; it stays private. The milestone is the proof; the method is not the write-up.

The transferable claim

Not "shipped a shadow feature". The capability the milestone demonstrates is getting a closed, ancient, source-less engine to accept a result computed entirely outside it and render with it correctly. That's the hard black-box problem, proven independently of whether the rest lands. When there's a stable build or a measured number, this log gets the update.
