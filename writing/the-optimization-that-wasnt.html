Reverse-engineering · Stormworks: Build and Rescue

The optimization that wasn't

An obviously-correct optimization: players build vehicles from many small blocks, so merge each vehicle's per-block collision shapes into one and win frame rate. Before building it, I reverse-engineered how the engine handles collision. The win didn't exist: the engine already does this.

Already done: the engine consolidates per body itself

Stormworks players assemble vehicles out of many small blocks, hundreds or thousands for a large build, each a discrete piece with its own collision shape, all simulated on a native physics engine.

The optimization sounds obviously correct: a thousand-block ship is a thousand collision shapes for the engine to handle every tick, so merge them into one combined shape and hand the engine a single object. Fewer shapes, less broad-phase work.

Why check before building

Merging collision geometry is a month of real work, validated against the game's own physics. That cost is the reason to check first: an optimization is only worth building if the thing it optimizes actually costs what you assume. So before writing any of it, one narrow question: does the engine really carry one collision shape per block into the physics step? The engine is a shipped binary with no source, so I reverse-engineered how it builds and registers collision for a completed body.

The finding

The engine already does the merge. When a body is built, it constructs a single compound collision shape for the whole body and registers one broad-phase proxy, one per body, not one per block. The broad phase the optimization was meant to relieve already sees one proxy, not a thousand.

The assumed model: what the optimization was built to fix
  block ──▶ shape ──▶ proxy ╮
  block ──▶ shape ──▶ proxy ├─▶ broad phase sees N proxies
  block ──▶ shape ──▶ proxy ├─▶ "merge them into one!"
  block ──▶ shape ──▶ proxy ╯

The actual model: what the engine already does
  block ─╮
  block ─┤
  block ─┼─▶ one compound shape ──▶ one broad-phase proxy per body
  block ─┤
  block ─╯    nothing left to merge

There was nothing to merge. The optimization would have reimplemented, at real cost, a consolidation the engine already performs internally. Best case it changes nothing; it cannot beat "already done."

Method withheld

This write-up states the conclusion about the engine (one compound shape and one broad-phase proxy per body) because the conclusion is the result. It omits how that was determined: the reverse-engineering technique, the way the engine's collision handling was inspected, any addresses, offsets, or RTTI, and the tooling.

Why this is published

The optimization was killed before a line of it was written, at the cost of a focused look at one question. Building the merge and then discovering it did nothing would have cost a month, plus the ongoing weight of maintaining a system that duplicates the engine. The output isn't a shipped fix; it's a decision not to build, backed by evidence rather than a guess.

The pattern

Verify the bottleneck exists before you optimize it. Sometimes the most valuable optimization is the one you prove you don't need to build. A null result is only worthless if you keep it to yourself; shared, it's the same discipline as a shipped fix, pointed at work that shouldn't happen.
