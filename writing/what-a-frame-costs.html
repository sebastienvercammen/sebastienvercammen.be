Reverse-engineering · Guild Wars 2 · independent research

What a frame costs

A ~14-year-old MMO, badly CPU-bound in crowds, with no way to see where a frame goes. I built its engine a live per-subsystem frame profiler, then used it to find that most of the shadow CPU cost is redundant recomputation repeated every frame. Diagnostic and self-attested: an observation and an opportunity, not a shipped win.

Guild Wars 2 is around twenty years old, on an in-house DirectX 11 engine. In a heavy crowd (a world boss, a busy city) the frame rate collapses on the CPU, not the GPU: on an RTX 4080 Super at 1440p the graphics card sits mostly idle while frame time climbs. The engine gives you no way to see where the time goes.

Shadow cost: an upper bound

Before optimizing shadow work, I measured the ceiling with the game's own shadow toggle: removing shadows entirely, same scene, took the frame from 75 to 109 fps.

ShadowsFrame rateGPU util
On75 fps43%
Off (A/B toggle)109 fps38%

That is the cost of removing the whole subsystem, an upper bound on what any shadow optimization could recover rather than a delivered gain. The ~4.1 ms delta matches the engine's own shadow-caster-render step at ~3.98 ms; two independent measurements agreeing on the same ~4 ms is what makes the number trustworthy.

Most of the work is redundant

The shadow pass rebuilds three per-cascade command lists, roughly 1.8 to 2.75 ms each, every single frame. But in a crowd the shadow-casting scene and view often haven't meaningfully changed between frames. The engine recomputes an identical result anyway.

Across those unchanged frames, about 76% of the shadow CPU work is redundant. Expensive work that has to happen is the cost of the feature; expensive work that repeats an identical result is waste, and waste is reachable.

A proof-of-concept

To test that the redundancy is reachable, I built a PoC that reuses the prior result when scene and view are unchanged and rebuilds only when they aren't. On a test frame it moved 10.58 ms → 10.04 ms: about 90 to 96.2 fps.

Test frameFrame timeFrame rate
Rebuild every frame10.58 ms~90 fps
Skip when unchanged (PoC)10.04 ms96.2 fps

A self-attested proof-of-concept on a specific frame, not a shipped feature: smaller than the upper bound, but a number I stood up and measured. It confirms the redundancy is genuinely reachable rather than theoretical.

Method withheld

This shows what was measured and concluded: the frame breakdown, the shadow-off upper bound, the redundancy figure, the PoC delta. It omits how: how the profiler attaches to the engine, how the skip-when-unchanged path is implemented, and every graphics-interop, resource, and binary-level specific that would make the tooling reproducible. The measurements are the proof; the method stays private.

What not to build

On inspection the biggest zones are mostly irreducible per-agent work that scales with crowd size and can't be cheaply cut. The profiler showed the shape of each zone's cost, not just its size, and steered the effort away from a dead end. A negative result is still a result.

Summary

  • The engine had no per-subsystem frame profiler; now it has one, and a stalling frame is legible.
  • Removing shadows entirely is worth ~4 ms: an upper bound, corroborated two independent ways, not a gain.
  • About 76% of the shadow CPU work is redundant recomputation of an identical result.
  • A PoC that skips the redundant rebuild proved a small, self-attested +7% on a real frame.
  • A separate analysis showed the biggest non-shadow zones are irreducible, and stopped the wrong optimization.

Measurement before optimization: build the instrument, find where the time actually goes, separate the cost that has to be paid from the cost being wasted, and hold to numbers you can reproduce.

Scope & framing

Private, unsolicited independent research: reverse-engineering a shipped game to understand where its frame time goes. There is no studio relationship, no engagement, and no credit; the results here are self-attested, measured on my own hardware. The profiler and the proof-of-concept are diagnostic instruments to test understanding, not a mod, a cheat, or anything shipped to players.
