Why one savanna tanked the frame rate

A map with a community reputation for lag really was ~42% slower than a comparable reserve. The cost concentrated in two level-of-detail tiers of a single grass mesh, 92% of the GPU bottleneck. A reversible cap recovered up to +79%, no gameplay change.

Studio Expansive Worlds / Avalanche Engine Apex · D3D11 Access shipped binary, no source Outcome disclosed · studio-acknowledged

Vurhonga Savanna had a community reputation for lag: the frame rate felt worse there than on other reserves, often enough to be folklore. Folklore isn't a bug report. Before any fix, the claim had to become a number.

143→256fps · +79% on the before/after gauge · gap vs a comparable map 42% → 5%

The measurement

A frame rate in isolation says nothing. Slow compared to what? To measure a deficit you need a control. I captured a matched baseline against Emerald Coast, a comparable forested reserve, under identical conditions.

Where the cost was

Attributing the frame's GPU cost stage by stage, the disparity did not spread across dozens of systems; it collapsed onto one. Two level-of-detail tiers of a single grass mesh were 92% of the GPU bottleneck. The cause was raw quantity: the savanna drew roughly seven times the grass the control did.

  One map was pushing seven times the grass geometry
  through the GPU, every single frame.

Not a mysterious engine problem. The savanna's authoring placed far more grass than a comparable map, and a single mesh, drawn at two LOD tiers, carried almost all of the extra cost.

Isolating the cost

A correlation is a hypothesis until you can switch the suspected cost off and watch the frame rate move by the predicted amount. Measured from the same 143 fps baseline on Vurhonga, each step fully reversible and with no change to what the player can do:

InterventionFrame rateChange from baseline
Baseline (Vurhonga, untouched)143 fpsreference
Native vegetation off228 fps+60%
Grass fully off215 fps+50%
Level-of-detail cap on the grass256 fps+79%

Grass off entirely bought +50%, a diagnostic probe rather than a fix. The last row is the result: a cap on how much grass actually draws recovered +79%, more than switching it off, while leaving the grass visibly present. With the cap in place, the 42% deficit against the control fell to roughly 5%.

Honest baseline caveat

The clean Vurhonga baseline drifted between roughly 143 and 175 fps across test phases, because the measurement instrumentation itself carries overhead that varied with how much was hooked. So the headline is a range, recovered up to +79%, closing a ~42% deficit to about 5%, rather than a single fixed number. The 143 → 256 fps figures are the matched before/after pair from one gauge, used only for that comparison, not as a claim about every machine.

Shown: the symptom, the measured deficit, the attribution, and the before/after numbers. Disclosure

The findings went to the studio through the same private channel as my other work on this title, and their QA engaged with them. Presented as a private acknowledgment rather than a shipped fix: the value here is the diagnosis and the measured recovery, not authorship of a released change.

The pattern

Three things transfer out of this, none specific to grass or to this engine.

A reputation for "lag" is a measurable claim, not a mood. Refusing to treat "this map feels slow" as true or false, and measuring it against a control until it became "42% slower," was the single most useful move.

The cost usually concentrates. Performance problems feel diffuse, but attribute a frame honestly and the damage collapses onto one asset drawn far too many times: here, two LOD tiers of one mesh at 92% of the bottleneck.

The best fix changes the frame budget, not the game. The cap that recovered the most did so by removing geometry the player was never going to perceive. The game is unchanged and the budget is real.
